# The Hollow — Story & Encounter Rules

These are authoring rules for new content in **The Hollow**.

## 1. Preserve the mystery

The campaign should feel connected long before the players fully understand how it is connected.

Prefer:

- recurring symbols
- similar architecture in places that should not know about each other
- NPC testimony that partially conflicts
- objects that gain meaning several sessions later
- apparently unrelated requests that turn out to serve the Hollow plot
- incomplete historical records
- consequences that prove a Hollow was influencing events before the players arrived

Avoid:

- exposition dumps explaining the setting
- an NPC who conveniently knows the entire truth
- revealing the final nature of the world early
- making every strange event immediately identifiable as "Hollow magic"

## 2. Connect side content back into the story

A preferred campaign pattern is:

1. the party encounters a local problem that appears independent
2. they can interact with it without knowing its importance
3. later evidence reveals that the problem was helping, feeding, hiding, containing, studying, or reacting to the Hollow
4. earlier details suddenly make more sense

Example structure only: a collector requests mundane objects, but the collection later proves to be components for a ritual. Do not copy that exact example automatically; use the principle.

The connection should be foreshadowed fairly enough that players can say "oh shit, that was connected" rather than "there was no way to know."

## 3. Each Hollow needs a unique gameplay identity

Do not repeat:

`town → missing person → forest → dead trees → ordinary dungeon → monster Catalyst`

for every chapter.

Each new Hollow should introduce at least one distinct behavior, rule, visual motif, social consequence, or encounter mechanic.

Possible categories include:

- memory
- time
- identity
- sound/silence
- duplicated spaces
- dreams
- emotion
- decay
- altered magic
- impossible geometry
- people forgetting an important person/place
- an effect that spreads through objects or rituals rather than terrain

These are idea categories, not canon until selected.

## 4. Catalysts should be memorable

A Catalyst is the thing sustaining a local Hollow.

A Catalyst should ideally be more than a generic boss with extra HP.

Good Catalyst encounters can involve:

- a boss whose mechanics express the Hollow's theme
- an artifact that must be disabled while enemies defend it
- environmental phases
- choices about what to destroy or preserve
- puzzle/combat hybrids
- a creature that is itself a victim of the Hollow
- a ritual with several points of failure

The players should be able to understand, at least by the end of the encounter, **why this thing is the Catalyst**.

## 5. Give clues jobs

Every important clue should answer at least one of these questions:

- Where should the party go?
- What happened here?
- Who is lying or hiding something?
- What rule does this Hollow follow?
- What is the Catalyst?
- How can the party survive/disable it?
- How does this connect to a previous chapter?
- What points toward the next chapter?

Avoid piles of spooky props that never matter.

## 6. Checks should not brick the story

Use skill checks to provide advantages, earlier understanding, optional information, safer routes, better loot, or alternate solutions.

Do not put the only path to the next required scene behind one roll.

For required information, use one or more of:

- automatic baseline clue + better detail on success
- multiple ways to discover the same conclusion
- failed check introduces a complication rather than ending progress

## 7. Encounters should have a reason to exist

Combat should usually do at least one additional job besides reducing HP:

- reveal a Hollow mechanic
- protect a clue
- interrupt an investigation
- force movement
- create a rescue objective
- introduce a recurring enemy/faction
- make players choose priorities
- demonstrate environmental danger
- change the state of a location

Use straightforward fights sometimes for pacing, but not as the default design for every room.

## 8. Encounter writeup format

For each prepared combat, provide concise DM-useful information:

### Trigger
What causes the encounter to begin?

### Enemies
Number and type of enemies, including any important stat adjustments.

### Terrain / mechanic
Anything beyond a flat empty battlefield.

### Tactics
Two to five bullets describing how enemies actually behave.

### Scaling
What to remove/add if the party is significantly weaker or stronger than expected.

### Reward / clue
What the party gains besides XP, if anything.

Do not write a page of tactical prose when five bullets will work.

## 9. NPC writeup format

For important NPCs, prefer:

- **Role**
- **Personality**
- **Wants**
- **Knows**
- **Doesn't know / is wrong about**
- **Hides**
- **How they can help**
- **Connection to larger plot**

Only write full dialogue when a specific line matters.

## 10. Scene writeup format

For major locations, prefer:

- short arrival description
- what is immediately visible
- interactables/discoverables
- checks and DCs
- NPCs present
- possible encounter trigger
- exits / next locations
- DM-only purpose of the scene

The DM should be able to run the scene by glancing at the notes.

## 11. Reward curiosity

Players who inspect strange symbols, talk to secondary NPCs, remember old clues, search rooms, or revisit assumptions should receive useful information or advantages.

Important optional discoveries can grant:

- a shortcut
- favorable positioning
- a boss weakness
- extra context
- treasure
- an NPC ally
- a future clue

## 12. Use personal hooks without making four separate campaigns

Tie player backstories into the same main plot where possible.

A good chapter may give one character a strong personal beat and the others smaller relevant hooks. Rotate focus over time.

Do not force every location to contain one custom subplot for each character.

## 13. Maintain momentum after a Catalyst

After defeating a Catalyst, include:

- an immediate visible consequence
- a moment to understand what changed
- at least one answer/revelation
- at least one unresolved question
- a concrete reason to move toward the next location

Avoid endings that amount to "you won; maybe look for another Hollow someday."

## 14. Campaign aesthetic

The broad visual mood established by Greymere is grounded dark fantasy: worn villages, forests, stone ruins, taverns, practical objects, and subtle supernatural corruption.

Veyrholt and later towns should feel like parts of the same world without being visually identical.

Use supernatural imagery more aggressively as the party gets deeper into a Hollow or uncovers something ancient.

## 15. DM usability beats literary prose

The campaign is played at a table through Wayfinder.

Favor:

- concise notes
- clear DCs
- visible triggers
- practical enemy behavior
- names the DM can scan quickly
- information grouped by scene

Avoid writing the chapter like a novel.

## 16. Track canon created by new chapters

When a chapter establishes a lasting fact about:

- Hollow behavior
- a faction
- a recurring symbol
- a player's backstory
- an important NPC
- world history
- the meaning of a prior clue

record it in the appropriate campaign document after implementation.

Do not leave important canon trapped only in implementation code or a one-off Codex transcript.
